## Section B　Matching

### Is the four-day week really the future of work?

**A)** The five-day week is so familiar that it feels like a law of nature, yet it is barely a century old. Before the 1920s, a six-day week was normal in most factories. Henry Ford is often credited with running a shorter week for his own workers, partly because he believed people with free time would buy more of his goods. Trade unions then turned the practice into a standard, and by the middle of the century the two-day weekend had become ordinary in industrialised countries.

**B)** The most-cited evidence for going further comes from Iceland, where public-sector workers took part in a series of trials between 2015 and 2019 involving about 2,500 people. Most participants moved from a forty-hour week to something closer to thirty-five or thirty-six hours without any reduction in pay. Researchers reported that productivity stayed the same or improved, and that workers described less stress and better balance between work and home.

**C)** A larger experiment followed in Britain in 2022, when sixty-one companies gave their staff an extra day off for six months. Fifty-six of them decided to keep the arrangement afterwards. Revenue was broadly unchanged, staff resignations fell sharply, and the number of people calling in sick dropped. For the firms involved, the trial was close to a commercial success.

**D)** There is, however, a serious limitation in this evidence, and it is one that supporters rarely mention. Companies volunteered to take part. They knew what was being tested and expected, on the whole, that it would work. Participants were overwhelmingly office workers whose output is easy to measure and easy to rearrange. Nothing in the design allows us to say what would happen if the same rule were imposed on a random sample of firms.

**E)** A more interesting explanation for the results is that a shorter week forces organisations to look at how their time is spent. Teams that took part typically cut the number of internal meetings, set blocks of time for concentrated work, and stopped expecting instant replies to messages. On this reading, the extra day off may matter less than the habits it makes people abandon, and those habits could be reformed without shortening the week at all.

**F)** Then there is the problem of measurement. Most trials rely on what workers say about their own productivity, and on how their managers judge it. Neither is neutral. When a company has announced proudly that it is running a four-day week, employees know what answer will please, and managers may be reluctant to report a fall. A trial that lasts six months may also capture little more than the enthusiasm of a new arrangement, an effect that tends to fade once the novelty has gone.

**G)** Some kinds of work simply cannot be compressed. A hospital ward needs a certain number of nurses on duty at any hour, and a class of thirty children needs a teacher in the room. In these settings, the choice is not between four days and five but between more staff and fewer days of service. That is a question of money rather than of management technique, which is why health and education unions have been cautious about the idea.

**H)** Small firms face a different set of difficulties. Covering a four-day week usually means hiring additional people or asking existing staff to work longer days, and neither is straightforward for a business with twelve employees. Scheduling becomes a puzzle, and the cost of getting it wrong falls on the owner.

**I)** For companies that sell directly to the public, the obstacle is expectation. Customers assume that someone will answer the phone on a Friday, and a supplier that closes for an extra day risks losing the order to a competitor who does not. In practice, many organisations that adopt a four-day week keep a small team on duty for five days, which raises the question of who does the extra day — and whether they are compensated for it.

**J)** The legal arrangements are rarely discussed in the campaign material but can be surprisingly complicated. Overtime rules, pension contributions and entitlements to holiday pay are all written with a five-day week in mind. Shortening the week can push staff over thresholds that neither they nor their employers intended, and the result may be disputes that take years to settle.

**K)** Finally, there is the question of who benefits. The evidence so far comes largely from professional employees with the bargaining power to demand better conditions. Workers in low-paid service jobs are less likely to be offered a shorter week and more likely to see their hours cut when demand falls — with their pay cut too. A reform that improves life for one group while leaving another worse off is not obviously progressive.

**L)** Some researchers argue that the argument has been framed in an unhelpful way. What workers value, they suggest, is not a fixed number of hours but a measure of control over when those hours are worked. Studies of flexible schedules often find larger gains in satisfaction, and smaller losses in output, than studies of shorter weeks. On this view the target should be autonomy, and the four-day week is only one of several ways of reaching it.

**M)** Public-sector experiments have also run into political difficulty. Governments that fund schools and hospitals must explain why one service is trying a shorter week while another is cutting staff. What begins as a productivity measure can quickly become a row about fairness, and several programmes have been quietly shelved.

**N)** The most useful lesson from all of this may have nothing to do with the number four. The trials show that a great deal of the working week is spent badly: in meetings that could be written down, in hours broken by messages, in time that is present but not productive. Whether or not the extra day ever arrives, the discovery that most of it could be reorganised is worth having on its own.

### Passage One

Many language learners believe that the way to sound fluent is to speak fast and to fill their speech with rare, difficult words. A new study from Waseda University in Japan suggests that this belief is mistaken. The opposite habit, using ordinary expressions that native speakers repeat every day, may matter far more.

The researchers recorded 102 Japanese speakers of English, each of whom delivered a short speech arguing a point of view. They then counted how often each speaker used common two- and three-word combinations, such as "in my opinion" or "on the other hand". Ten experienced judges, who did not know the purpose of the experiment, listened to the recordings and rated how fluent each speaker sounded. To rule out general smoothness as the explanation, the team also measured how fast each person spoke, how long they paused, and how often they corrected themselves. These factors were then allowed for in the analysis.

Two results stood out. First, the smoothness of delivery was by far the strongest predictor of how fluent a speaker was judged to be, explaining 61% of the differences among the ratings. Smoothness here means the speed and flow of speech. Second, even after smoothness had been taken into account, the frequent phrases still made a small but real difference of about 0.8%. Rare and complicated expressions, by contrast, had almost no effect at all on how the speakers were rated.

The lesson for learners is practical. Instead of hunting for impressive vocabulary, they should master the everyday phrases that come up again and again in conversation. Saying "I agree with the idea", the researchers note, sounds natural, while "I agree the idea" does not, although the second version is no less ambitious. Teachers who spend class time on difficult words may therefore be preparing students for the wrong test.

### Passage Two

Most people assume that a bad working day is caused by bad luck, a difficult task, or too little sleep. A study from the University of Toronto suggests a more specific explanation. On any given day, the sharpness of your thinking rises and falls, and that movement quietly decides how much you get done.

The researchers followed the same students for twelve weeks rather than comparing different people. Each day, the students took short tests of how quickly and accurately they could think, and reported their goals, their mood, their sleep and their workload. Because every participant was measured against himself or herself, the results could not be put down to temperament. The same person simply performed better on some days than on others.

The size of the effect surprised the team. Being sharper than usual was worth roughly forty extra minutes of productive work in a day, and the gap between a person's best and worst days came to about eighty minutes. Sharpness also changed what people attempted. On clear-headed days they set themselves larger goals and, more importantly, finished them.

What drives the daily change turns out to be partly within reach. Students did better after more sleep than usual, and better earlier in the day, with performance declining as the hours passed. Feeling motivated helped; being in a low mood hurt. Overwork produced a result that many managers will recognise. A long day in itself went with higher sharpness, as people rose to the immediate demand, but sustained overwork pulled the measure down.

The practical advice is therefore modest and slightly uncomfortable. Sleep, avoid long stretches without a break, and treat mood as a factor in performance rather than a private matter. The lead author offers a further suggestion that fits awkwardly with office culture: on a day when the mind will not cooperate, accept it. Pushing through fog, the data imply, costs more than it achieves.
